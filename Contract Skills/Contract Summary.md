# Singapore Contract Summary

## Purpose and how this differs from the review skills

A summary answers "what does this contract actually say and require", quickly and
accurately, for someone who has not read it or needs a refresher before a meeting or
decision. It is descriptive, not evaluative: it does not rank severity, does not require a
Singapore statute or case citation for every point, and does not recommend whether to sign.
That is the job of the applicable review skill (`Sales and Purchase Agreement.md`,
`Tenancy Agreement.md`, `Employment Agreement.md`, or `General Contract Agreement.md`).

An experienced lawyer preparing a summary still cannot help noticing what looks unusual,
onerous or missing -- real practice is to flag these briefly under **Notable or unusual
terms** at the end, without turning the summary into a review. If the client's actual need
is "should I be worried about this", say so and point to the applicable review skill instead
of quietly turning this summary into a partial risk analysis.

This skill produces a professional-style work product; it does not confer practising status
or constitute legal advice on whether to proceed.

## Intake and method

1. Identify the document version being summarised, its date, and whether it is a draft,
   signed original, or a specific amendment. Note the parties' capacity (e.g. company vs
   individual, landlord vs tenant, employer vs employee) as stated in the document.
2. Read the entire document, including schedules, annexures, definitions and any documents
   expressly incorporated by reference. A summary built from only the main body when the
   commercial terms live in a schedule (e.g. a tenancy's rent schedule, an SPA's completion
   deliverables list) is an inaccurate summary, not a shorter one.
3. Identify the contract's substance (sale/purchase, tenancy, employment, services, loan,
   NDA, etc.) -- this determines which type-specific emphasis in the table below to apply,
   and which review skill to point to if the reader wants risk analysis.
4. Quote key figures, dates and defined terms verbatim from the source rather than
   paraphrasing them loosely -- a summary that rounds "S$10,500" to "about ten thousand
   dollars" or restates a notice period imprecisely is a source of real error downstream.
5. Where information a summary would normally contain is missing, absent, or only in an
   unavailable schedule, say so explicitly (e.g. "rent review mechanism: not stated in the
   supplied document"). Do not infer or assume a typical/market term that is not actually
   in the text.

## What the summary must contain

### 1. Snapshot

A short table, not prose:

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| Contract type | (as identified from substance, not just the title) |
| Parties | Full legal names and capacity/role of each party |
| Effective/commencement date | |
| Term/duration | Fixed term, at-will, or ongoing until terminated; renewal terms if any |
| Governing law | |
| Dispute resolution | Litigation (courts), arbitration (institution and seat), or mediation-first |
| Key monetary value | Price, rent, salary, fees, or contract value, with currency |

### 2. Subject matter and purpose

One short paragraph in plain English: what is being bought/sold/leased/performed/employed
for, and the commercial context if stated (e.g. "a 12-month residential tenancy of a unit at
[address], for S$3,500/month, with a 3-month deposit").

### 3. Key obligations by party

For each party, a short list of what they must actually do -- deliver, pay, perform,
maintain, notify, or refrain from. Distinguish a firm obligation ("shall") from a
discretionary right ("may") -- summarising a right as though it were a duty misleads the
reader about what is actually required.

### 4. Payment terms

Amounts, currency, timing/triggers, method, late-payment consequences, deposits, and any
price-adjustment or escalation mechanism, quoted from the text.

### 5. Key dates and milestones

A dated table wherever the contract has more than one or two -- signing, commencement,
milestones, renewal/notice deadlines, expiry, and any long-stop or condition-precedent date.
For a rolling or evergreen arrangement with no fixed end, say so rather than leaving the
field blank.

### 6. Termination and exit

Notice periods (state the exact wording, e.g. "2 months' written notice" not "short notice"),
termination triggers (for cause, for convenience, insolvency), cure periods, and what happens
on exit (return of property, final payments, surviving obligations, deposit return process).

### 7. Liability and risk-allocation snapshot

State what the contract says about liability caps, exclusions, indemnities and
insurance requirements -- as a factual summary of the mechanism (e.g. "Party A's liability is
capped at 12 months' fees, excluding indemnities under clause 9"), not an assessment of
whether that allocation is fair, enforceable or typical. That assessment belongs in a review.

### 8. IP, confidentiality and data, if present

Ownership of work product/IP, confidentiality obligations and their duration, and any
personal-data handling terms, stated factually.

### 9. Notable or unusual terms

A short list -- typically no more than five items -- of anything an experienced Singapore
lawyer would flag as worth the reader's attention: an unusually one-sided clause, an absent
term the reader would normally expect (e.g. no diplomatic clause, no liability cap, no
dispute-resolution clause), an internal inconsistency between clauses, or a defined term used
inconsistently. State it as an observation, not a verdict: "Clause 4 permits forfeiture of
the entire deposit for any reason, with no proportionality mechanism -- this is more
one-sided than typical Singapore tenancy drafting" rather than "this clause is unenforceable."
If any item here looks material enough to affect the reader's decision, recommend a full
review using the applicable skill rather than expanding this list into one.

### 10. Missing information

List documents, schedules, or facts referenced but not supplied, and any summary field above
that could not be completed because the contract is silent on it.

## Type-specific emphasis

| Contract type | Summarise with particular attention to |
|---|---|
| Sale/purchase (property, shares, business assets, goods) | Price and payment structure (deposit, instalments, completion), conditions precedent, completion date/long-stop date, what exactly is being transferred (and what is excluded), and post-completion warranty/claim periods. |
| Tenancy/lease | Rent and deposit, permitted use, repair/maintenance allocation, renewal option and rent-review mechanism, and early-termination/diplomatic-clause position. |
| Employment | Remuneration structure (base, bonus, benefits), notice period, probation, restrictive covenants (non-compete/non-solicit) and their duration/scope, and termination grounds. |
| Services, consultancy, SaaS/licensing | Scope of services/deliverables, service levels or acceptance criteria, fees and payment triggers, term and renewal, and data/IP ownership. |
| Loan, financing or guarantee | Principal, interest, repayment schedule, security given, guarantor obligations, and events of default/acceleration triggers. |
| NDA / confidentiality | What information is covered, permitted disclosures/exceptions, duration of the obligation (including post-termination survival), and remedies stated for breach. |

## Style discipline

- Write for a reader who has not read the contract and will not read it after this summary
  -- but never invent a fact, figure, or party that is not in the text to make the summary
  read more smoothly.
- Use plain English. Where a technical term is unavoidable (e.g. "long-stop date",
  "novation", "diplomatic clause"), use it but do not assume the reader already knows it.
- Keep the summary proportionate to the contract: a two-page tenancy agreement does not need
  the same length of summary as a fifty-page SPA with schedules. Do not pad with boilerplate
  observations to make a short contract's summary look more thorough than it is.
- Preserve clause/page references for every material fact stated, so the reader (or a
  follow-up reviewer) can find it in the source document without re-reading the whole thing.
- Never present this summary as a substitute for legal advice, and never let "Notable or
  unusual terms" quietly become a severity-ranked risk register -- if that is what is
  actually needed, say so and hand off to the applicable review skill.

## Final quality check

Confirm every figure, date and defined term matches the source document verbatim; every
party's obligations are correctly attributed (not swapped between parties); the snapshot
table and the narrative sections do not contradict each other; missing information is stated
as missing, not silently omitted; and the "Notable or unusual terms" section has not grown
into an unrequested risk assessment. If the contract's substance changed mid-review (e.g. a
side letter converts a services agreement into something closer to employment), reflect that
in the snapshot rather than leaving the original classification unchanged.
